Long before a roaster ever touches a green coffee, the most consequential decisions about its flavour have already been made. Varietal and terroir set the stage, but processing — what happens to the cherry in the hours and days after picking — often does more to shape the final cup than any single choice you make at the drum. For buyers matching lots to roast profiles and end markets, understanding processing isn't academic. It's the difference between a coffee that flatters your house blend and one that fights it.

This guide walks through the three families of processing you'll encounter most — washed, natural, and honey — how each shapes flavour and consistency, and the questions worth asking before you commit to a container.

## What Processing Actually Does

A coffee cherry is fruit. Inside the pulp and mucilage sits the seed we roast. Processing is simply the method of separating that seed from the fruit and drying it to a stable moisture level, usually around 10 to 12 percent. The variable that matters is how much fruit stays in contact with the seed, and for how long, during drying.

More contact means more sugars and organic acids migrating into and fermenting around the bean. Less contact means a cleaner separation. Every named process is really a point on that spectrum, and every point carries a trade-off between clarity and intensity, between predictability and character.

## Washed: Clarity and the Cost of Consistency

In the washed (or wet) method, the skin and pulp are removed mechanically, and the remaining mucilage is broken down — traditionally through a controlled fermentation in tanks, then rinsed away — before the bean is dried as clean parchment. Because the fruit is stripped early, the cup tends to foreground the coffee's intrinsic acidity, structure, and origin character rather than added fruit sweetness.

For a lot of buyers, washed coffees are the reliable backbone of a portfolio. They tend to be transparent: you taste the varietal and the terroir with fewer variables laid over the top. That transparency cuts both ways. A washed process is unforgiving of a mediocre lot, because there's no jammy fruit to hide behind. But when the raw material is good, the result is a bright, articulate cup that roasts predictably and holds up well in milk-based drinks.

Washed processing is also the most water- and infrastructure-intensive of the three, and the most standardised, which is part of why it delivers consistency batch to batch — a real consideration if you're buying at volume for a blend that has to taste the same all year.

## Natural: Intensity, Body, and Higher Variance

The natural (or dry) process is the oldest method and, in principle, the simplest: the whole cherry is dried intact, fruit and all, before the dried husk is removed. During that extended drying, sugars and compounds from the fruit permeate the seed, and a slow fermentation develops inside the cherry.

The payoff is intensity. Well-executed naturals show heavier body, lower perceived acidity, and pronounced fruit — berry, stone fruit, sometimes a wine-like or fermented note. For certain markets and roast styles, that expressiveness is exactly the selling point.

The risk is variance. Drying whole cherry evenly is difficult; do it poorly and you get uneven, over-fermented, or "boozy" defects, along with more cup-to-cup inconsistency within the same lot. Naturals also behave differently in the roaster — the residual sugars make them prone to scorching and faster development, so they often reward a gentler approach. If you're buying naturals, you're buying character, and you should price the additional QC and roast-development attention into your plan.

## Honey and Pulped Natural: The Middle Path

Honey processing sits deliberately between the two. The skin is removed, as in the washed method, but some or all of the sticky mucilage is left on the parchment to dry. "Honey" refers to that tacky mucilage, not to any flavour of honey.

You'll see honeys graded by how much mucilage is retained and how it's dried — white, yellow, red, and black honeys, roughly from least to most fruit contact and slowest drying. More mucilage and slower, shadier drying push the cup toward natural-like sweetness and body; less pushes it toward washed-like clarity. Done well, a honey delivers a rounded, syrupy sweetness with more structure than a natural and more depth than a washed — a genuinely useful middle option for single origins and premium blends.

The catch is control. Honey processing demands careful, attentive drying; the exposed mucilage is vulnerable to mould and uneven fermentation if turning and airflow aren't managed. The best honeys come from producers with the drying discipline to match the ambition.

## How Each Shapes Your Roast and Market

Match the process to the job. A washed lot suits a roaster who wants transparency, tight consistency across a year of production, and a coffee that performs in espresso and milk. A natural suits a filter-forward market or a customer base that prizes fruit-driven intensity and is forgiving of batch character. A honey often finds a home where you want distinctive sweetness and body without going fully to the wild end of a natural — a strong pick for a rotating single origin or a signature blend component.

Roast development follows the same logic: cleaner washed lots tolerate a more standard profile, while sugar-rich naturals and honeys usually need a gentler charge and more attentive first-crack management to avoid tipping and baked flavours.

## What to Ask Before You Commit

Before you sign for a lot, get specific with your supplier. Ask which process was used and, for honeys and naturals, how the drying was managed — raised beds or patio, days on the bed, whether the coffee was shaded. Ask about moisture content and water activity at export, since both predict how the coffee will hold in storage. Ask how consistent the process is across the lot and across harvests, and request a representative pre-shipment sample rather than a cherry-picked one. For naturals especially, ask what defect screening looks like. The answers tell you as much about the producer's rigour as about the coffee itself.
